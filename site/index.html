About

FrontierSat is Calgary’s first student-built satellite, made by the CalgaryToSpace team. More than 100 students have worked on it, mainly from UCalgary’s Schulich School of Engineering and Faculty of Science. It lifted off on a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Vandenberg, California, on May 3, 2026.

What FrontierSat is for

Its science target is STEVE, a narrow ribbon of purple light in the night sky that researchers have found is not an aurora. Its main instrument, the Mini Plasma Imager, measures winds of charged gas high above Earth. It also tests a new boom of 3D-printed carbon fibre, stowed for launch and designed to reach 1 143 mm when it opens.

It carries two cameras. The main camera takes 640 × 480 pictures and is there mostly to watch the boom unfold; pictures of Earth and the aurora are its other jobs. The attitude camera, a second and smaller one, helps the satellite work out which way it is pointing, and takes 512 × 512 pictures.

How a picture gets here

The satellite stores each picture as a file and sends it down in small packets, often over several passes. Volunteer ground stations in the SatNOGS network record the passes, CalgaryToSpace decodes the packets, and this site puts each file back together.

What the status means

Checked
Every byte arrived and matches the satellite’s own fingerprint of the file.
Complete, not checked
The whole picture arrived, but it can’t be checked against the satellite: the satellite sent no fingerprint of the file, or a few bytes outside the picture are missing.
Partly missing
Part of the file never came down. A main-camera picture is shown only down to its first gap, because the rest of it can’t be read past one. An attitude-camera picture shows grey bands where rows are missing.
Cut short on board
The satellite stopped the capture early and stored only part of the frame, so the top of the picture is all there is. The copy here can still match the satellite’s fingerprint of that shortened file.

Files that couldn’t be shown

These picture files are known from the satellite’s records or packets, but none of the picture can be shown.

    Times and positions

    Times are given in Calgary time first, then in UTC. The time a picture was taken comes from its file name, the camera’s own records, or the satellite’s reply to the command that took it. Some pictures only have a date. Most of these times are readings of the satellite’s own clock, which has run up to a couple of minutes fast; where the data shows how far off it was, the time is corrected for it.

    Positions are estimates: the point below the satellite when the picture was taken, worked out from the satellite’s most recent published orbit before then. Place names come from OpenStreetMap.

    Edits from the team

    Team members can give a picture a title, notes, a place, the people involved and tags, and can correct its time or position. Edits are made on the team’s own copy of the site and appear here when that copy is next published. The worked-out values are never overwritten, and every edited value is marked with the name of the person who changed it.
